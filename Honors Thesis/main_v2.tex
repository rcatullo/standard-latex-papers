\abstract{We give a brief overview of the theory of affine Grassmannians and background on Frobenius splittings. In particular, we discuss Frobenius splitting methods relating to Schubert varieties and connect this to a discussion of splitting for Schubert cells of variants of the affine Grassmannian. \section{Introduction}
\subsection{Motivation}
The main motivation for the results in this paper is toward the study of the moduli stack of principal $G$-bundles over a curve $X$, denoted $\Bun_G$ (when $X$ is clear from context). Given a smooth projective curve $X$ over $k$ and a smooth affine group scheme $G$ over $X$, the $2$-functor $\Bun_G \colon \Aff_k^{\op} \to \Grpd$ sends a $k$-algebra $R$ to the category of principal $G$-bundles $\fane$ on $X_R := X \times_k \spec R$. Perhaps the most enlightening example is when $G=\GL_r$ in which case $\Bun_{\GL_r}$, which is usually denoted $\Bun_r$, sends $R$ to the category of $\GL_r$-bundles on $X_R$. If we fix the trivial representation $\rho \colon \GL_r \to \End(k^r)$ we can form the associated category of rank $r$ vector bundles $\fane \times_{\rho} k^r = (\fane \times k^r)/\GL_r$ on $X_R$, and therefore $\Bun_r$ can be seen to be the moduli stack of rank $r$ vector bundles on $X$.

We can equivalently define it as the Hom $2$-functor $\rHom(X, \bg)$ where $\bg$ is the classifying stack. For a $k$-algebra $R$, the $R$-points is the category $\Hom_R(X_R, \bg) \simeq \bg(X_R)$, which is defined as the category of principal $G$-bundles on $X_R$. This stack has some nice properties like being algebraic and locally of finite type over $k$.

The affine Grassmannian $\Gr$ and its variants provide powerful tools to study $\Bun_G$ for the following reason. If we pick a point $x \in X(k)$ and a local coordinate $t$ of the complete local ring $\hat{\calo}_{X,x} \cong k[[t]]$ at $x$, then we can identify
\[\Gr_{\GL_r, x} = \{\fane \text{ is a vector bundle on } X \text{ and } \beta \colon \fane\vert_{X\setminus \{x\}} \simeq \calo^{\oplus r}_{X\setminus \{x\}} \text{ a trivialization}\}\]
It is apparent that this maps to the underlying set of points on $\Bun_r$ by $(\fane, \beta) \mapsto \fane$, which we want to upgrade to a morphism $\Gr_{\GL_r, x} \to \Bun_r$. In fact we can define $\Gr_{G,x}$ for more general smooth affine group schemes $G$, and construct a morphism
\[u_x \colon \Gr_{G,x} \rightarrow \Bun_G\]
Such a map is called a \textit{uniformization map}, and this particular morphism is called the one-point uniformization map. Following the idea of Beilinson and Drinfeld, we can trivialize away from collections of points $x = \{x_1,\ldots,x_n\}$ that we let "wander" along the curve to produce the range Grassmannian $\GrRan$, whose points are $(x, \fane, \beta)$ where $\beta$ is a trivialization of a vector bundle $\fane$ over $X$ away from $x$.

If we quotient this space by the equivalence relation $(x,\fane, \beta) \sim (x', \fane', \beta')$ whenever there is an isomorphism of vector bundles $\fane \simeq \fane'$ which restricted away from $x \cup x'$ is compatible with the trivializations $\beta, \beta'$, we roughly get the rational Grassmannian $\GrRat$. This space upgrades our morphism
\[u_{\mathrm{Rat}} \colon \GrRat \rightarrow \Bun_G\]
which is called the \'adelic uniformization map.

We consider the Frobenius morphism $F \colon X \to X$ which is the identity on points, but on the structure sheaf $F^{\#}\colon \calo_X \to F_* \calo_X$ is the $p$th power map. We can make sense of this more generally for prestacks on $\Aff_k$, and in particular for $\Bun_G$ and $\GrRat$. We say a space is Frobenius split if $\calo_X \to F_* \calo_X$ splits as $\calo_X$-modules. This is a particularly nice property since a number of nice consequences arise, one being vanishing of higher cohomology of ample line bundles on split spaces (without twisting!). The goal is the following conjecture.
\begin{conj}
    For $G$ a simple, simply connected smooth affine group scheme, the stack $\Bun_G$ is Frobenius split.
\end{conj}
We will see that we can understand the relevant geometry, i.e. rigidified line bundles $\Pic^e(\Bun_G)$ on $\Bun_G$, in terms of $\Pic^e(\GrRan/\mathrm{Ran})$ by adelic uniformization. We understand this Picard group well in special cases, and can build line bundles from analogues of Schubert cells as in the usual Grassmannian. The fact that Schubert varieties are well-known split spaces gives some loose motivation for the conjecture.

\section{Affine Grassmannians}

The purpose of this section is to review the standard definitions and relevant constructions as presented in the literature (\cite{Zhu2016}, \cite{Faltings2003}), and in the particular setting of positive characteristic. 

Recall that---for $G$ a semisimple algebraic group over $k$, $B$ a Borel subgroup---the space $G/B$ is a flag variety consisting of finitely many $B$-orbits parameterized by the Weyl group $W$. In particular,
\begin{equation}\label{bruhat}
    G/B = \bigsqcup_{w \in W} BwB/B = \bigsqcup_{w \in W} \interior C_w
\end{equation}
is the \textit{Bruhat decomposition} of $G/B$ into Bruhat cells. These are cells in the sense of algebraic topology, i.e. $C_w := BwB/B \simeq k^{\ell(w)}$. Poincar\'e duality then gives information on the cell decomposition, in particular that the top dimensional cell (fundamental class) is unique and corresponds to the unique longest element $w_0 \in W$ by the previous observation. 

The closures $X_w := \overline{\interior C_w}$ equipped with the reduced subscheme structure are \textit{Schubert varieties}. In fact $X_w = \bigsqcup_{x \leq w} C_x$ where $\leq$ is the Bruhat order on the Weyl group $W$. The idea of the affine Grassmannian is to define affine analogues of these objects, which allows one to develop the theory in more generality.

We give a concise account as in \cite{Faltings2003}, but a more detailed description of the general theory can be found in \cite{Zhu2016}, \cite{Sorger2000}. Central to this study is the following definitions of loop groups.

\begin{defn}
    Let $G$ be a smooth affine algebraic group scheme. The positive loop group $L^+G$ is the affine group scheme given by $L^+G(R) = G(R[[t]])$. The strict positive loop group $L^{++}G$ is the kernel of the restriction map $L^+G \to G$. 
\end{defn}
    

\begin{defn}
    The negative loop group $L^-G$ is the ind-affine group scheme with $L^-G(R) = G(R[t^{-1}])$. The strict negative loop group $L^{--}G$ is the kernel of the restriction map $L^-G \to G$.
\end{defn}

\begin{defn}
    The loop group $LG$ is the ind-affine group with $LG(R) = G(R((t)))$.
\end{defn}

The loop group $LG$ is perhaps most easily motivated. As $D^*_R = \spec R((t))$ is the punctured disk, the $R$-valued points of $LG$ are sections $D^*_R \to G$ defining loops in $G$. 

\begin{defn}
    The affine Grassmannian of $\GL_n$ is the presheaf $\Gr_{\GL_n}$ which assigns to every $k$-algebra $R$ the set of projective $R[[t]]$-modules $L \subseteq R((t))^n$ such that
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item $L$ is finitely generated, and
        \item $L \otimes_{R[[t]]} R((t)) = R((t))^n$
    \end{enumerate}
\end{defn}

A module $L$ satisfying (i) and (ii) in the above definition is sometimes referred to as an $R$-family of lattices.

Analogously, we can define $\Gr_{\SL_n}(R)$ as follows.

\begin{defn}\label{GrSLn}
    The affine Grassmannian of $\SL_n$ is the presheaf $\Gr_{\SL_n}$ which assigns to every $k$-algebra $R$ the set of projective $R[[t]]$-modules $L \subseteq R((t))^n$ such that
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item $L$ is finitely generated, and
        \item $\det(L) = R[[t]]$
    \end{enumerate}
\end{defn}

For more general $G$, the definition is not quite an obvious generalization but we will see that it is equivalent.

\begin{defn}
    Let $G$ be a smooth affine group scheme over $k$. The affine Grassmannian $\Gr_G$ of $G$ is the presheaf whose $R$-points are given by
    \[\Gr_G(R) := \left\{(\fane,\beta) \;\Big\vert\; \begin{array}{c}
    \fane \text{ is a } G \text{-torsor on } D_R \\ 
    \beta \colon \fane\vert_{D_R^*} \simeq \fane^0\vert_{D_R^*} \text{ is a trivialization} 
    \end{array} \right\}\]
\end{defn}

This presheaf is represented by an ind-scheme, ind-of finite type over $k$. If $G$ is reductive then $\Gr_G$ is ind-projective (see \cite{Zhu2016}). 

Note that for $G = \GL_n$ the $R$-points are $(\fane, \beta)$ where $\fane$ is a $\GL_n$-torsor on $D_R$, or equivalently vector bundle (locally free sheaf) of rank $n$ on $\spec R[[t]]$. Any such quasicoherent sheaf is given by a projective $R[[t]]$-module $L$ and $\beta$ therefore specifies an isomorphism $L \otimes_{R[[t]]} R((t)) \simeq R((t))^n$.

This space admits a natural action by $LG$. An $R$-point $g \in G(R((t)))$ is an automorphism of the trivial $G$-torsor $\fane^0 \vert_{D^*_R}$ on $D^*_R = \spec R((t))$, and thus $g$ acts on $(\fane, \beta)$ by sending it to $(\fane, \beta^* g)$. The action is transitive and the stabilizer of the origin $(\fane^0, \id)$ is $L^+G$. Then the following theorem, taking these facts for granted, follows from a souped up generalization of orbit-stabilizer.

\begin{prop}[Coset Description]\label{cosetdescription}
    The affine Grassmannian $\Gr_G$ can be identified with the fpqc quotient $[LG/L^+G]$.
\end{prop}

We can define a natural very ample line bundle on the affine Grassmannian $\Gr_G$. There is an explicit description in the case $G = \SL_n$, but in generality it is constructed for the so-called Sato Grassmannian associated to a Tate vector space $V$. 

Then letting $V = k((t))^n$ with the $t$-adic topology allows one to define it for $\GL_n$. Any faithful $\calo$-linear representation $\rho \colon G \to \GL_n$  induces a locally closed embedding $\Gr_G \to \Gr_{\GL_n}$ by Proposition 1.2.6 in \cite{Zhu2016}. The pullback along $\rho$ then determines a very ample line bundle on $\Gr_G$.

We will proceed with the explicit description in the special case of $\SL_n$ using Definition \ref{GrSLn}. Let $L \in \Gr_{\SL_n}(R)$ be an $R$-family of lattices, and 
\[M = t^{-1} R[t^{-1}]^n\subset R[[t]]^n \oplus M = R((t))^n\] 
an $R$-module. We have a $2$-term complex of $R$-modules
\begin{equation}
    0 \to L \oplus M \xrightarrow{\gamma} R((t))^n \to 0
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation*}
    \gamma(v,w) = (0,w) + \beta(v)
\end{equation*}
where $\beta \colon L \to R((t))^n$ is the natural inclusion. Recall that the determinant of a finite projective $R$-module is an invertible module given by the top exterior power. We can take the determinant cohomology of this complex to obtain a line bundle. That is, dividing both terms by 
\[D = t^N R[[t]]^n \oplus M\]
for $N \gg 0$ ensures they are finite and projective as $R$-modules, so we can take the determinant of the complex to get
\[\det\nolimits_R (R((t))^n/D) \otimes \det\nolimits_R (L \oplus M/D)^{-1} = \det\nolimits_R(L)^{-1}\]
Varying $L$ gives us a line bundle.
\begin{equation}
    \fanl_{\det} = \det\nolimits_R(L)^{-1}
\end{equation}

In fact, we have also shown the following result for $G = \SL_n$ by letting $\vartheta = \det(\gamma)$, but which also holds in more generality (Proposition 2.4.1 in \cite{Zhu2016}).

\begin{prop}
    There is a global section $\vartheta \in \Gamma(\Gr_G, \fanl_{\det})$ invariant under $L^-G$, whose zero set is denoted by $\Theta$.
\end{prop}

The action of $LG$ extends to a projective action on $\fanl_{\det}$ due to the above procedure, but the stabilizer of any fixed lattice ($L^+G$) acts honestly (\cite{Faltings2003}).

\subsection{Affine Schubert Schubert Varieties}
We will see that the line bundle constructed above actually has a rather explicit connection to the generalization of Shubert subvarieties of $\Gr$.

Unless otherwise noted, we assume that $k$ is algebraically closed and write $\Gr$ for $\Gr_G$ when no confusion will arise. We use the notation in Section 1.2.

There is a canonical bijection
\begin{equation}\label{dblcosetbij}
    G(\calo) \backslash G(F) / G(\calo) \simeq \coweight / W \simeq \coweight^+
\end{equation}
given as follows. Fix embeddings $T \subset B \subset G$. Then a coweight $\mu \in \coweight$ gives rise to a map $\mu \colon F^{\times} \to T(F) \subset G(F)$. For a given choice of uniformizer $t$ of $F$, which induces $F \simeq k((t))$, we denote its image by $\mu(t) = t^{\mu} \in G(F)$, regarded as an element of $LG(k) = G(k((t))) \simeq G(F)$.

By Proposition \ref{cosetdescription} this induces an element of $\Gr_G(k)$ which we still denote $t^{\mu}$, but this is independent of choice of uniformizer since two choices are equivalent under the action of $L^+G(k)$.

Then we have the Cartan decomposition
\begin{equation}\label{cartan}
    G(F) = \bigsqcup_{\mu \in \coweight^+} G(\calo) t^{\mu} G(\calo)
\end{equation}
whose double cosets depend neither on $t^{\mu}$ nor choice of embedding $T \subset G$, and therefore induces (\ref{dblcosetbij}).

With this in hand, let $\fane_1, \fane_2$ be two $G$-torsors over $D = \spec \calo$ and
\[\beta \colon \fane_1\vert_{D^*} \simeq \fane_2\vert_{D^*}\]
be an isomorphism over $D^* = \spec F$. Since $D$ is contractible $\fane_1, \fane_2$ are trivializable, so we can pick isomorphisms $\phi_1 \colon \fane_1 \simeq \fane^0$ and $\phi_2 \colon \fane_2 \simeq \fane^0$. 

We can then construct an automorphism of the trivial $G$-torsor over $D^*$ by $\phi_2 \circ \beta \circ \phi_1^{-1} \in \Aut(\fane^0 \vert_{D^*})$ and therefore an element of $G(F)$. Different choices of $\phi_1$ (resp. $\phi_2$) differ this element by left (resp. right) multiplication by an element of $G(\calo)$, and therefore $\beta$ gives rise to a well-defined element 
\begin{equation}
    \inv \beta \in G(\calo) \backslash G(F) / G(\calo) \simeq \coweight^+
\end{equation}
sometimes called the \textit{relative position of} $\beta$. In terms of the Cartan decomposition (\ref{cartan}), $\inv \beta$ is the \textit{unique} dominant coweight such that
\[\phi_2 \circ \beta \circ \phi_1^{-1} \in G(\calo) t^{\inv \beta} G(\calo)\]

We can bootstrap this definition to define it over $\spec R$. First, if $K$ is some non-algebraically closed containing $k$ then we can pass to an algebraic closure $\bar{K}/K$ to define
\[\inv \beta_{\bar{K}} \in G(\bar{K}[[t]]) \backslash G(\bar{K}((t))) / G(\bar{K}[[t]])\]
which is independent of our choice of $\bar{K}$. Therefore, $\inv \beta = \inv \beta_{\bar{K}}$ is well-defined.

Now let $\fane_1,\fane_2$ be two $G$-torsors over $D_R$, and 
\[\beta \colon \fane_1\vert_{D_R^*} \simeq \fane_2 \vert_{D_R^*}\]
Then for each $x \in \spec R$, we can base change to $k(x)$ to obtain $\fane_1\vert_{D_{k(x)}}, \fane_2\vert_{D_{k(x)}}$ and an isomorphism
\[\beta_{k(x)} \colon \fane_1\vert_{D_{k(x)}^*} \simeq \fane_2 \vert_{D_{k(x)}^*}\]
where $k(x)/k$ is not necessarily algebraically closed. Then $\inv_x \beta = \inv \beta_{k(x)} = \inv \beta_{\bar{k(x)}}$ is well-defined.

The following is Proposition 2.1.4. in \cite{Zhu2016}.
\begin{prop}\label{zhu2.1.4}
    Let $X = \spec R$. Given $\mu \in \coweight^+$, the set of points
    \[X_{\leq \mu} := \{x \in X \mid \inv_x \beta \leq \mu\}\]
    is Zariski-closed in $X$.
\end{prop}
The following definition is our analogue of a Schubert variety in the context of the affine Grassmannian.
\begin{defn}
    Let $\mu \in \coweight^+$ be a coweight. We define the \textit{spherical Schubert variety} as the closed subset
    \[\Gr_{\leq \mu} := \{(\fane, \beta) \in \Gr_G \mid \inv \beta \leq \mu\} \subset \Gr_G\]
    endowed with the reduced scheme structure. 
\end{defn}
The condition $\inv \beta \leq \mu$ is shorthand for $\inv_x \beta \leq \mu$ for all $x \in \spec R = X$, which is the closed condition $X_{\leq \mu} = X$ by Proposition \ref{zhu2.1.4}. By analogy, this enables us to define the affine Schubert cell.
\begin{defn}
    The \textit{affine Schubert cell} associated to a coweight $\mu \in \coweight^+$ is the subset
    \[\Gr_{\mu} := \{(\fane, \beta) \in \Gr_G \mid \inv \beta  = \mu\} = \Gr_{\leq \mu} \setminus \bigcup_{\lambda < \mu} \Gr_{\leq \lambda}\]
\end{defn}
By Proposition \ref{zhu2.1.4}, $\Gr_{\mu}$ is an open subset of $\Gr_{\leq \mu}$.
\begin{prop} The following two statements hold.
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item $\Gr_{\mu}$ forms a single $L^+G$-orbit and is a smooth quasi-projective variety of dimension $(2\rho, \mu)$.
        \item $\Gr_{\leq \mu}$ is the Zariski closure of $\Gr_{\mu}$, and so is a projective variety.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{prop}

If $\mu$ is a minimal nonzero dominant coweight with respect to the Bruhat order, i.e. for any positive root $\alpha$ the inner product $(\alpha, \mu) \leq 1$, then $\mu$ is called \textit{minuscule}.
\begin{lem}
    If $\mu$ is minuscule, then $\Gr_{\leq \mu} = \Gr_{ \mu} \simeq G/P_{\mu}$ where $P_{\mu}$ is the parabolic subgroup of $G$ corresponding to $\mu$.
\end{lem}
This is exactly our definition of a partial flag variety in the usual setting. 

A nonzero dominant coroot $\mu$ is \textit{quasi-minuscule} if it is not minuscule and $(\alpha, \mu) \leq 2$ for all positive roots $\alpha$. If we assume the derived group $G_{der}$ of $G$ is simple this element is unique, denoted $\theta$, and is minimal in $\coweight^+ \setminus 0$ under the Bruhat order. The root $\theta^{\vee}$ associated to $\theta$ is the highest root. 

After our discussion of Frobenius splitting in Section 3, we will return to discuss a kind of Demazure resolution associated to the spherical Schubert variety.

\subsection{Beilinsin-Drinfeld Grassmannians}

The content of this subsection is the main substance of the conjecture at hand. The idea of these Grassmannians is to allow $G$-torsors over excised graphs of collections of points that wander across our curve. We will see it has a deep connection with the moduli stack of principal $G$-bundles on a curve. 

Let $X$ be a reduced connected curve (but not necessarily smooth) over $k$ and $x \in |X|$ a smooth closed point. Let $G$ be a smooth affine group scheme over $X$.
\begin{defn}
    We define the presheaf $\Gr_{G,x}$ whose $R$-points are given by
    \[\Gr_{G,x}(R) := \left\{(\fane,\beta) \;\Big\vert\; \begin{array}{c}
        \fane \text{ is a } G\text{-torsor on } X_R \\
        \beta \colon \fane\vert_{X_R^*} \simeq \fane^0\vert_{X_R^*} \text{ is a trivialization} 
    \end{array}\right\}\]
\end{defn}
In fact, we can see this is just a reinterpretation of the affine Grassmannian by noting that for $G_x := G \otimes_X \calo_{X,x}$, there is a natural isomorphism
\begin{equation}\label{resiso}
    \text{res} \colon \Gr_{G,x} \xrightarrow{ \sim } \Gr_{G_x}
\end{equation}
The proof that this is an isomorphism is slightly technical so we omit it.

Now we allow the point to move. For a point $x \in X(R)$, let $\Gamma_x $ denote the graph of $x \colon \spec R \to X$. 
\begin{defn}
    We define the prestack $\Gr_{G,X}$ whose $R$-points are given by
    \[\Gr_{G,X}(R) := \left\{(x, \fane, \beta) \;\Big\vert\; \begin{array}{c}
        x \in X(R), \quad\fane \text{ is a } G\text{-torsor on } X_R \\
        \beta \colon \fane\vert_{X_R\setminus \Gamma_x} \simeq \fane^0\vert_{X_R\setminus \Gamma_x} \text{ is a trivialization} 
    \end{array}\right\}\]
\end{defn}
We can allow multiple points to move as well using a similar idea. Let $I$ be a finite nonempty set, and $x = (x_i)_{i \in I} \in X^I(R)$ a point. Let
\[\Gamma_x := \bigcup_{i \in I} \Gamma_{x_i}\]
denote the union of the graphs of $x_i \colon \spec R \to X$ (as closed subschemes of $X_R$). Then the following is also referred to as the Beilinsin-Drinfeld Grassmannian.

\begin{defn}
    We define the presheaf $\Gr_{G, X^I}$ over the self product of curves whose $R$-points are given by
    \[\Gr_{G, X^I}(R) := \left\{(x, \fane, \beta) \;\Big\vert\; \begin{array}{c}
        x \in X^I(R), \quad\fane \text{ is a } G\text{-torsor on } X_R \\
        \beta \colon \fane\vert_{X_R\setminus \Gamma_x} \simeq \fane^0\vert_{X_R\setminus \Gamma_x} \text{ is a trivialization} 
    \end{array}\right\}\]
\end{defn}
Let $q_I \colon \Gr_{G,X^I} \to X^I$ denote the structure map. As a first account of the properties, which we present without proof but are found in Theorem 3.1.3 in \cite{Zhu2016}, the presheaf $\Gr_{G,X^I}$ is represented by an ind-scheme, ind-of finite type over $X^I$. It is ind-projective if $G$ is reductive.

We can also define analogues of loop groups and produce a similar coset description. Namely, for $x \in X^I(R)$ let $\hat{\Gamma}_x$ denote the formal completion of the graph $\Gamma_x$ in $X_R$. There is the canonical map $\pi \colon \hat{\Gamma}_x \to X_R$.

Recall this is like a "formal thickening" of the topological algebra $A_x$ of its ring of functions. 

Then the Beilinsin-Drinfeld positive loop group is
\[(L^+G)_{X^I} := \{(x,\beta) \mid x \in X^I(R), \beta \in G(\hat{\Gamma}_x)\}\]
which is representable by a scheme affine over $X^I$.

To rigorously define global loop groups, we need the notion of a "punctured formal completion" as we did with disks. 
\begin{lem}
    The formal scheme $\hat{\Gamma}_x$ is ind-affine. Let $A_x$ be the topological algebra of its ring of functions, so $\hat{\Gamma}_x = \mathrm{Spf} A_x$. Let $\hat{\Gamma}'_x = \spec A_x$ which gives a canonical map $p \colon \hat{\Gamma}_x \to \hat{\Gamma}'_x$. Then there is a unique morphism $i \colon \hat{\Gamma}'_x \to \hat{\Gamma}_x$ such that $\pi = ip \colon \hat{\Gamma}'_x \to X_R$.
\end{lem}
The above lemma essentially says that $\hat{\Gamma}'_x$ is the underlying scheme without the formal thickening, and therefore our punctured equivalent would be $\interior{\hat{\Gamma}}_x := \hat{\Gamma}'_x \setminus \hat{\Gamma}_x$.

The Beilinsin-Drinfeld global loop group is then defined as
\[(LG)_{X^I} := \{(x, \beta) \mid x \in X^I(R), \beta \in G(\interior{\hat{\Gamma}}_x)\}\]
Then we have an analogue of the coset description given in Proposition \ref{cosetdescription}.
\begin{prop}
    The Beilinsin-Drinfeld Grassmannian $\Gr_{G,X^I}$ can be identified with the fpqc quotient $[(LG)_{X^I} / (L^+G)_{X^I}]$.
\end{prop}
Let's analyze the cases $I = \{1\}, \{1,2\}$. In the first case we can write $\Gr_{G,X}$ or $\Gr_X$ when $G$ is apparent from context. Locally, at any $x \in X$ we have an isomorphism
\[\Gr_X \vert_x \simeq \Gr_{G,x} \simeq \Gr_{G_x}\]
where the latter isomorphism is (\ref{resiso}). However, globally it is not true that $\Gr_X \simeq \Gr_{G} \times X$ in general. Instead, $\Gr_X$ is a twisted product $X \tilde{\times} \Gr_G$ in the sense that there is an $\Aut(D)$-torsor $\hat{X} \to X$ for which 
\[\Gr_X \simeq \hat{X} \times^{\Aut(D)} \Gr_G\]
There is a second isomorphism as well. We can define an $\Aut^+(D)$-torsor $\hat{X}^+ \to X$ for which 
\[\Gr_X \simeq \hat{X}^+ \times^{\Aut^+(D)} \Gr_G\]
See (3.1.11) in \cite{Zhu2016} for the definition of these torsors. The second isomorphism allows us to define
\[\Gr_{\leq \mu, X} := X \tilde{\times} \Gr_{\leq \mu} := \hat{X}^+ \times^{\Aut^+(D)} \Gr_{\leq \mu}\]
as a closed subscheme of $\Gr_X$. On the level of points, we can see
\[\Gr_{\leq \mu, X} = \{(x,\fane, \beta) \in \Gr_X \mid \inv_x \beta \leq \mu\} \subset \Gr_X\]
Now suppose $I = \{1,2\}$ and denote the Beilinsin-Drinfeld Grassmannian in this case by $\Gr_{X^2}$. Let $\Delta \colon X \to X^2$ be the diagonal embedding. 
\begin{prop}\label{factorX2}
    There are canonical isomorphisms
    \[\Delta \colon \Gr_X \simeq \Gr_{X^2} \times_{\Delta} X, \qquad c\colon \Gr_{X^2} \vert_{X^2 \setminus \Delta} \simeq (\Gr_X \times \Gr_X)\vert_{X^2 \setminus \Delta}\]
\end{prop}
\begin{proof}
    We prove this statement as the details are not too bad. Consider the first isomorphism. On the level of $R$-points, $\Gr_{X^2}$ is given by tuples $ ((x,y), \fane, \beta)$ such that $\fane$ is a $G$-torsor over $X_R$ admitting a trivialization over $X_R \setminus (\Gamma_x \cup \Gamma_y)$. 
    
    The structure map sends $((x,y), \fane, \beta) \mapsto (x,y)$, so if the image lies on the diagonal then $x = y$. Thus our $R$-points are given by $((x,x), \fane, \beta)$ where $\fane$ admits a trivialization over $X_R \setminus \Gamma_x$. But this is exactly $\Gr_X$.

    For the second isomorphism, consider again the functor of $R$-valued points $((x,y), \fane, \beta)$. Since we are off-diagonal, $x,y$ are distinct. Then
    \[\beta \colon \fane\vert_{(X_R \setminus \Gamma_x) \cap (X_R \setminus \Gamma_y)} \simeq \fane^0\vert_{(X_R \setminus \Gamma_x) \cap (X_R \setminus \Gamma_y)}\]
    We first can define a map
    \[\Gr_{X^2} \times_{X^2} \spec R \to \Gr_X \times_{X,x} \spec R\]
    by sending $((x,y), \fane, \beta)$ to the $G$-torsor $\fane_x$ on $X_R$ given by gluing $\fane\vert_{X_R \setminus \Gamma_y}$ to $\fane^0\vert_{X_R \setminus \Gamma_x}$ along $\beta$ above (on the restriction to $X_R \setminus (\Gamma_x \cup \Gamma_y)$). Since $X_R \setminus \Gamma_x$ and $X_R\setminus \Gamma_y$ cover $X_R$, this is a well-defined torsor on the whole space. This also comes with a natural trivialization $\beta_x$ over $X_R \setminus \Gamma_x$ since $\fane_x \vert_{X_R \setminus \Gamma_x} = \fane^0\vert_{X_R \setminus \Gamma_x}$.

    We similarly have a map $\Gr_{X^2} \times_{X^2} \spec R \to \Gr_X \times_{X,y} \spec R$. Together, they define a map
    \[c\colon \Gr_{X^2} \vert_{X^2 \setminus \Delta} \to (\Gr_X \times \Gr_X)\vert_{X^2 \setminus \Delta}, \quad ((x,y), \fane, \beta) \mapsto ((x, \fane_x, \beta_x), (y, \fane_y, \beta_y))\]
    To see this is an isomorphism, suppose we are given $(x, \fane_x, \beta_x)$ and $(y,\fane_y, \beta_y)$. We can glue $\fane_x$ and $\fane_y$ along $\beta_y^{-1}\beta_x$ over $X_R \setminus (\Gamma_x \cup \Gamma_y)$, over which by definition it is equipped with a trivialization.
\end{proof}

There is a natural generalization of this statement to general Beilinsin-Drinfeld Grassmannians which we state but leave the proof to Theorem 3.2.1 in \cite{Zhu2016}. This is sometimes referred to as the \textit{Factorization Property} of these Grassmannians. 
\begin{thm}[Factorization Property]\label{factorization}
    Let $G$ be a smooth affine group scheme over $X$. 
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item For every $I$, there is a unit section $e^I \colon X^I \to \Gr_{X^I}$ given by the trivial $G$-torsor.
        \item For every $\phi \colon J \twoheadrightarrow I$, let $\Delta(\phi) \colon X^I \to X^J$ be the corresponding diagonal embedding sending $(x_i)_{i \in I}$ to $(x_{\phi(j)})_{j \in J}$. Then there is a canonical isomorphism
        \begin{equation}
            \Delta(\phi) \colon \Gr_{X^J} \times_{X^J} X^I \simeq \Gr_{X^I}
        \end{equation}
        Then we can naturally define the map on Grassmannians by composing the above isomorphism with fiber projection
        \[\Delta(\phi) \colon \Gr_{X^I} \to \Gr_{X^J}\]
        We also denote this $\Delta(\phi)$ by abuse of notation.
        \item Partition $J = \sqcup_{i \in I} J_{i}$ where $J_i = \phi^{-1}(i)$, and let $X^{(\phi)} \subset X^J$ denote the open subset of tuples $(x_j)_{j \in J}$ such that $x_j, x_j'$ are disjoint if $\phi(j) \neq \phi(j')$. That is, the coordinates in $J_i$ are disjoint from the coordinates in $J_{i'}$ for every $i' \neq i$. Then there is a canonical isomorphism (factorization isomorphism)
        \begin{equation}
            c_{\phi} \colon \Gr_{X^J} \times_{X^J} X^{(\phi)} \simeq \left(\prod_{i \in I} \Gr_{X^{J_i}}\right) \times_{X^J} X^{(\phi)}
        \end{equation}
    \end{enumerate}
\end{thm}
Analyzing statement (iii) above, it becomes clear why this is a natural generalization of the factorization map in Proposition \ref{factorX2}. Taking the fiber product with $X^{(\phi)}$ ensures we are "off-diagonal" in the sense that graphs of coordinates in different preimages under $\phi$ are disjoint. 

This allows us to glue the trivial torsor restricted to the complement of our $J_i$ graphs to the given torsor over the complement of everything else, yielding an element of $\Gr_{X^{J_i}}$ for each $i \in I$.

The factorization statement becomes much cleaner when we organize all of our $\Gr_{X^I}$ into a single object. This is the motivating idea behind the \textit{Ran Grassmannian}.

\subsection{The Ran Grassmannian}

\begin{defn}
    We define the Ran space $\Ran(X)$ of $X$ as the presheaf that sends $R$ to the colimit $\varinjlim_I X^I(R)$, where the limit is taken in the category of presheaves over the category of finite nonempty sets under surjections.
\end{defn}

Despite the name, $\Ran(X)$ is not a space since the colimit is taken over a non-filtered category. It is not a sheaf either, even for the \'etale topology. 

\begin{defn}
    The Ran Grassmannian $\Gr_{G,\Ran(X)}$ is the prestack whose $R$-valued points are given by
    \[\Gr_{G,\Ran(X)}(R) := \left\{(x, \fane,\beta) \;\Big\vert\; \begin{array}{c}
    x \in \Ran(X)(R),\qquad \fane \text{ is a } G \text{-torsor on } X_R \\ 
    \beta \colon \fane\vert_{X_R \setminus \Gamma_x} \simeq \fane^0\vert_{X_R \setminus \Gamma_x} \text{ is a trivialization} 
    \end{array} \right\}\]
\end{defn}

We'll denote this by $\Gr_{\Ran}$ when $G, X$ is clear from context. Now we turn to the factorization property.

$\Ran$ has a nice semigroup structure given by union of points:
\[\Ran \times \Ran \to \Ran, \quad ((x_i)_{i \in I}, (x_j)_{j \in J}) \mapsto (x_k)_{k \in I \sqcup J}\]
Let $(\Ran \times \Ran)_{disj} \subset \Ran \times \Ran$ denote the open subpresheaf consisting of $(x_i)_{i \in I}$ and $(x_j)_{j \in J}$ with $x_i \cap x_j = \emptyset$ disjoint for all $i \in I, j \in J$. One similarly defines $(\Ran \times \Ran \times \Ran)_{disj}$. 
\begin{thm}
    There is a canonical isomorphism
    \begin{equation}
        a\colon \Gr_{\Ran} \times_{\Ran} (\Ran \times \Ran)_{disj} \simeq (\Gr_{\Ran} \times \Gr_{\Ran})\vert_{(\Ran \times \Ran)_{disj}}
    \end{equation}
    compatible with the unit section $e_{\Ran} \colon \Ran \to \Gr_{\Ran}$ and satisfying a natural cocycle condition over $(\Ran \times \Ran \times \Ran)_{disj}$.
\end{thm}

This is in fact equivalent to Theorem \ref{factorization}.


\section{Frobenius Splitting}\label{frobsplit}

We give an overview of the basic definitions and properties of Frobenius splittings based on the exposition given in \cite{Brion2004}. Throughout this section, we assume $X$ is a scheme over a field $k$ of characteristic $p > 0$.

\begin{defn}
    Let $\fanf$ be a presheaf of commutative rings on $X$. The (absolute) Frobenius morphism on $\fanf$ is the natural transformation $F_{\fanf} \colon \fanf \rightarrow \fanf$ sending sections $s \mapsto s^p$.

    If $\fanf = \calo_X$, then the Frobenius morphism on $X$ refers to the map $F \colon X \to X$ which is the identity on points, but for which $F^\# \colon \calo_X \to F_* \calo_X$ is the $p$th power map.
\end{defn}

Then the definition of Frobenius splitting is as one would expect. We state these definitions and results for a scheme $X$, but they can be generalized to a presheaf valued in commutative rings on $X$.

\begin{defn} We say that a scheme $X$ is \textit{Frobenius split} if the $\calo_X$-linear map $F^{\#}$ splits, i.e. there exists an $\calo_X$-linear map
    \[\varphi \colon F_* \calo_X \rightarrow \calo_X\]
    such that $\varphi \circ F^{\#} $ is the identity on $\calo_X$. A closed subscheme $Y \subset X$ is \textit{compatibly split} if there is a splitting $\varphi$ of $X$ such that 
    \[\varphi(F_* \cali_Y) \subset \cali_Y\]
    A collection of closed subschemes $Y_1,\ldots,Y_m$ are \textit{compatibly split} if there is a splitting $\varphi$ of $X$ such that each subscheme is compatibly split with respect to $\varphi$.
\end{defn}
Note that a splitting $\varphi$ is equivalent to an endomorphism $\varphi \colon \calo_X \to \calo_X$ such that $\varphi(f^p g) = f \varphi(g)$ and $\varphi(1) = 1$. The first condition means $\varphi \in \End_{\calo_X}(F_* \calo_X, \calo_X)$ and since $\varphi(F^{\#}(f)) = \varphi(f^p) = f\varphi(1)$ the composition is the identity if and only if $\varphi(1) = 1$.

    Further, for a closed subscheme $Y \subset X$ we always have $\cali_Y \subset \varphi(F_* \cali_Y)$ since for any section $f$ of $\cali_Y$ we have $f = \varphi(f^p) \in \varphi(F_*\cali_Y)$. Thus compatibly split can be restated as $\varphi(F_* \cali_Y) = \cali_Y$.

One of the most important consequences is that the higher cohomology of all ample line bundles on a split scheme $X$ vanishes. 

Compare this with Serre vanishing, which says for an ample line bundle $\fanl$ all higher cohomology vanishes \textit{after twisting sufficiently high}, i.e. $H^{i > 0}(X,\fanl^{\otimes v}) = 0$ for all $v \gg 0$. For split schemes, higher cohomology vanishes without having to twist the bundle.

\begin{lem}\label{bklem1.2.6}
    Let $\fanl$ be a line bundle on $X$, i.e. an invertible $\calo_X$-module. Then
    \[F^* \fanl \simeq \fanl^p \quad\textit{ and }\quad F_*F^* \fanl \simeq \fanl \otimes_{\calo_X} F_* \calo_X \]
\end{lem}
\begin{proof}
    Recall
    \[F^* \fanl = F^{-1} \fanl \otimes_{F^{-1} \calo_X} \calo_X\]
    Since $F$ is the identity map on points and the $p$th power map on on sheaves, 
    \[F^* \fanl = \fanl \otimes_{\calo_X} \calo_X\]
    where $\calo_X$ is a module over itself by the $p$th power map, i.e. $f.g = f^pg$. Then define the isomorphism $\fanl \otimes_{\calo_X} \calo_X \rightarrow \fanl^p$ by $\sigma \otimes g \mapsto \sigma^p g$, which is $\calo_X$-linear as $\sigma f \otimes g = \sigma \otimes f^p g \mapsto \sigma^p f^p g = (\sigma f)^p g$. The second isomorphism follows from the projection formula.
\end{proof}

\begin{lem}\label{bklem1.2.7}
    Let $\fanl$ be a line bundle on a split scheme $X$. Then
    \begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
        \item If $H^i(X, \fanl^v) = 0$ for fixed $i$ and all $v \gg 0$, then $H^i(X, \fanl) = 0$.
        \item If $Y \subset X$ is compatibly split and the restriction $H^0(X, \fanl^v) \rightarrow H^0(Y, \fanl\vert_Y^v)$ is surjective for all $v \gg 0$, then $H^0(X, \fanl) \rightarrow H^0(Y, \fanl\vert_Y)$ is surjective.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{lem}
\begin{proof}
    (i) Let $\varphi$ split $X$, and note that $\id \otimes \varphi$ splits the map
    \[\id \otimes F^{\#} \colon \fanl \rightarrow \fanl \otimes_{\calo_X} F_*\calo_X\]
    The the induced map in cohomology
    \[H^i(\id \otimes F^{\#}) \colon H^i(X, \fanl) \rightarrow H^i(X, \fanl \otimes_{\calo_X} F_* \calo_X)\]
    is split, hence injective. But
    \[H^i(X, \fanl \otimes_{\calo_X} F_* \calo_X) \simeq H^i(X, F_* F^* \fanl) \simeq H^i(X, F_* \fanl^p) \simeq H^i(X, \fanl^p)\]
    by the previous lemma and the fact that $F$ is finite, hence affine (for the isomorphism on the right as abelian groups). Hence we have a split injection $H^i(X, \fanl) \rightarrow H^i(X, \fanl^p)$, which inductively implies $H^i(X, \fanl)$ is a direct summand of $H^i(X, \fanl^{p^v})$ for any $v > 0$.

    (ii) We have a commutative diagram
    \[\begin{tikzcd}
	{H^0(X, \fanl)} & {H^0(X, \fanl^p)} \\
	{H^0(Y, \fanl\vert_Y)} & {H^0(Y, \fanl^p\vert_Y)}
	\arrow[from=1-1, to=1-2]
	\arrow[from=1-1, to=2-1]
	\arrow[from=1-2, to=2-2]
	\arrow[from=2-1, to=2-2]
\end{tikzcd}\]
Since $Y$ is compatibly split, the horizontal arrows are compatibly split as well. Therefore if $H^0(X, \fanl^p) \rightarrow H^0(Y, \fanl^p\vert_Y)$ is surjective then so is $H^0(X, \fanl) \rightarrow H^0(Y, \fanl\vert_Y)$.
\end{proof}
This leads us to the vanishing result.
\begin{thm}
    Let $X$ be a proper scheme over an affine scheme, and let $\fanl$ be an ample invertible sheaf on $X$.
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item If $X$ is split, $H^i(X, \fanl) = 0$ for all $i > 0$.
        \item If $Y$ is compatibly split, then $H^0(X,\fanl) \rightarrow H^0(Y, \fanl\vert_Y)$ is surjective and $H^i(Y, \fanl\vert_Y) =0 $ for all $i > 0$. This implies $H^i(X, \cali_Y \otimes \fanl) = 0$ for all $i > 0$.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{thm}
\begin{proof}
    By Serre vanishing $H^i(X, \fanl^v) = H^i(X, \cali_Y \otimes \fanl^v) = H^i(Y, \fanl\vert_Y^v) = 0$ for $v \gg 0$. The short exact sequence
    \[0 \rightarrow \cali_Y \otimes \fanl^v \rightarrow \fanl^v \rightarrow \fanl\vert_Y^v \rightarrow 0 \]
    yields
    \[0 \rightarrow H^0(X, \cali_Y \otimes \fanl^v) \rightarrow H^0(X, \fanl^v) \rightarrow H^0(Y, \fanl\vert_Y^v) \rightarrow H^1(X, \cali_Y \otimes \fanl^v) \rightarrow \ldots\]
    It follows $H^0(X, \fanl^v) \rightarrow H^0(Y, \fanl\vert_Y^v)$ is surjective, and by Lemma \ref{bklem1.2.7} the result follows.
\end{proof}
\subsection{Splitting Criteria}
Define the evaluation map on sheaves
\[\veps \colon \rHom_{\calo_X}(F_* \calo_X, \calo_X) \rightarrow \calo_X, \quad \varphi \mapsto \varphi(1)\]
From now on we assume all Homs are taken as $\calo_X$-modules unless otherwise stated. Note the domain above is a coherent $F_* \calo_X$-module, and since $F$ is finite there is a unique $F^! \calo_X$ such that (\cite{Vakil2025} Exercise 17.1.I.)
\[\rHom(F_* \calo_X, \calo_X) \simeq F_*(F^! \calo_X)\]
If $X$ is regular then $F$ is flat, and Grothendieck-Serre duality for a finite flat morphism yields
\[F^! \calo_X \simeq \rHom(F^* \omega_X, \omega_X)\]
Note that Lemma \ref{bklem1.2.6} then implies $F^! \calo_X \simeq \rHom(\omega_X^p, \omega_X) \simeq \omega_X^{1-p}$. Recall the trace map $\tau \colon F_* \omega_X \to \omega_X$ defined as in Grothendieck-Serre duality. Then by these remarks, the evaluation map
\[\veps \colon F_* \rHom(F^* \omega_X, \omega_X) \simeq \rHom(\omega_X, F_* \omega_X) \rightarrow \calo_X\]
can be identified with
\[\hat{\tau} \colon F_* \omega_X^{1-p} \simeq \rHom(\omega_X, F_* \omega_X) \rightarrow \rEnd(\omega_X) \simeq \calo_X, \quad u \mapsto \tau \circ u\]
\begin{rem}If $X$ is a nonsingular variety, then one can show that $\hat{\tau}$ above is given locally at any closed point $x$ by
\[\hat{\tau}(f(dt_1 \wedge \ldots \wedge dt_n)^{1-p}) = \tr(f)\]
for all $f \in \calo_{X,x} \subset k[[t_1,\ldots,t_n]]$ and 
\[\tr\left(\sum_{\ul{i}}f_{\ul{i}} t^{\ul i}\right) := \sum f_{\ul{i}}^{1/p} t^{\ul{j}}\] 
where the summation is over $t^{\ul j} = t^{(j_1,\ldots,j_n)} = t_1^{j_1} \cdots t_n^{j_n}$ such that $\ul i = \ul{p-1} + p \ul j$.
\end{rem}
Then we have the following characterization of split varieties.
\begin{thm}\label{bkthm1.3.8}
    Let $X$ be a nonsingular variety. Then $\varphi \in H^0(X, \omega_X^{1-p})$ splits $X$ iff $\hat{\tau}(\varphi) = 1$.
\end{thm}
In particular, it is a necessary condition for a nonsingular variety $X$ to be split that $\omega_X^{1-p}$ has a nonzero section. We have the following consequence, which will be of use in the section on Schubert varieties.
\begin{prop}\label{bkprop1.3.11}
    Let $X$ be a nonsingular variety of dimension $n$. If $X$ is complete and there is $\sigma \in H^0(X, \omega_X^{-1})$ with divisor of zeros 
    \[(\sigma)_0 = Y_1 + \ldots + Y_n + Z\]
    where $Y_1,\ldots,Y_n$ are prime divisors intersecting transversally at a point $x$ and $Z$ is effective with support disjoint from $x$, then $\sigma^{p-1} \in H^0(X,\omega_X^{1-p})$ splits $X$ compatibly with $Y_1,\ldots,Y_n$.

    Conversely, if $\sigma \in H^0(X, \omega_X^{-1})$ is such that $\sigma^{p-1}$ splits $X$, then the subscheme of zeros of $\sigma$ is compatibly split (in particular reduced).
\end{prop}

\subsection{Splitting of Schubert Varieties}
We follow the notation of the general theory of algebraic groups. Namely, let $G$ be a connected, simply connected, semisimple algebraic group over an algebraically closed field $k$ of characteristic $p > 0$. Let $B$ be a fixed Borel subgroup, $T$ a maximal torus, $U$ the unipotent radical of $B$ such that $B = TU$. Let $W = N(T)/T$ be the Weyl group. Varieties are assumed integral. We let $\{s_1,\ldots,s_{\ell}\} \subset W$ be the set of simple reflections generating $W$.

We have the bijective correspondance between standard parabolic subgroups $G \supset P \supset B$ and $I \subset \{1,\ldots,\ell\}$ by
\[I \leftrightarrow P_I := \bigsqcup_{w \in W_I} BwB\]
where $W_I \leq W$ is the subgroup generated by $\{s_{i} \colon i \in I\}$. We denote the standard minimal parabolic subgroups $P_i := P_{\{i\}} = B \sqcup Bs_iB$.

Recall the Bruhat cell $C_w = BwB/B$ and the Schubert variety $X_w$, the closure of $C_w$ in $G/B$ with the reduced subscheme structure. The boundary is the union of all codimension one Schubert subvarieties in $X_w$
\[\del X_w = X_w \setminus C_w = \bigsqcup_{x < w} C_x\]
where $x < w$ is the Bruhat-Chevally order on $W$.

Let $w \in W$ and write $w = s_{i_1}\cdots s_{i_n}$ be a reduced expression in terms of simple reflections. Then by the Bruhat decomposition, $BwB = Bs_{i_1}B\cdots Bs_{i_n}B$. It follows that
\[X_w = P_{i_1}\cdots P_{i_n}/B\]
The product map $P_{i_1} \times \ldots \times P_{i_n} \to X_w$ is invariant under the action of $B^n$ given by $(p_1,\ldots,p_n) \odot (b_1,\ldots,b_n) = (p_1 b_1, b_1^{-1} p_2 b_2, \ldots, b_{n-1}^{-1} p_n b_n)$.
\begin{defn}[BSDH Variety]
    Let $\mfw = (s_{i_1},\ldots,s_{i_n})$ be an ordered sequence of simple reflections in $W$, also called a word in $W$. Define the \textit{Bott–Samelson–Demazure–Hansen (BSDH) variety} $Z_{\mfw}$ as the orbit space
    \[Z_{\mfw} := P_{\mfw}/B^n\]
    where $B^n$ acts on $P_{\mfw} := P_{i_1} \times \ldots \times P_{i_n}$ as above.
\end{defn}
The goal of this section is to show that $Z_{\mfw}$ is split. We give a few important first properties, and then construct its canonical line bundle in order to use some of the theory developed in Section \ref{frobsplit}.

We can put a smooth projective variety structure on $Z_{\mfw}$ such that the orbit map $\pi_{\mfw} \colon P_{\mfw} \to Z_{\mfw}$ is a locally trivial principal $B^n$-bundle. For details see \cite{Brion2004} Section 2.2, which gives a commutative diagram below whose horizontal arrows are closed embeddings and vertical arrows are $B^n$-bundles.
\[\begin{tikzcd}
	{P_{\mathfrak{w}}} & {G^n} \\
	{Z_{\mathfrak{w}}} & {(G/B)^n}
	\arrow["{\phi_n}", from=1-1, to=1-2]
	\arrow["{\pi_{\mathfrak{w}}}"', from=1-1, to=2-1]
	\arrow["\pi", from=1-2, to=2-2]
	\arrow["{\phi_{\mathfrak{w}}}"', from=2-1, to=2-2]
\end{tikzcd}\]
Namely, the horizontal maps are given by
\[\phi_n(p_1,\ldots,p_n) := (p_1,p_1p_2,\ldots,p_1\cdots p_n), \quad \phi_{\mfw}[p_1,\ldots,p_n]=(p_1B, p_1p_2B, \ldots, p_1\cdots p_n B)\]
where $[p_1,\ldots,p_n]$ denotes the $B^n$-orbit of the corresponding $P_{\mfw}$ point. In particular, $Z_{\mfw}$ is smooth since $P_{\mfw}$ is smooth.

The left multiplication of $P_{i_1}$ on the first factor of $\phi_{\mfw}$ gives $Z_{\mfw}$ the structure of a $P_{i_1}$-variety. The projection of $\phi_{\mfw}$ on the last factor gives a $P_{i_1}$-equivariant morphism 
\[\theta_{\mfw} \colon Z_{\mfw} \rightarrow G/B, \quad \theta_{\mfw}[p_1,\ldots,p_n] := p_1\cdots p_n B\]
Since $P_i = B \sqcup Bs_i B$ and $Bs_i B \simeq U_{\alpha_i} \times B$ where $U_{\alpha_i}$ is the root subgroup of $U$ corresponding to $\alpha_i \leftrightarrow s_i$, we see
\[\interior Z_{\mfw} := ((Bs_{i_1}B) \times \ldots \times (B s_{i_n} B))/B^n\]
is an open subset isomorphic to the affine space $\prod_{j=1}^n U_{\alpha_{i_j}}$. We can see if $\mfw$ gives a reduced expression in $W$ that $\theta_{\mfw}(Z_{\mfw}) = P_{i_1}\cdots P_{i_n}/B = X_w$ is a Schubert variety, where $w= s_{i_1}\cdots s_{i_n}$. Moreover $\theta(\interior Z_{\mfw}) = Bs_{i_1}B\cdots Bs_{i_n} B/B = C_w$ is the Bruhat cell, and $\theta_{\mfw}$ restricts to an isomorphism $\interior Z_{\mfw} \to C_w$. Thus $\theta_{\mfw}$ provides a desingularization of $X_w$.

We can take subsequences of $\mfw_J := (s_{i_{j_1}}, \ldots, s_{i_{j_m}}) \subset (s_{i_1}, \ldots, s_{i_n}) = \mfw$ for $J \subset I$ and construct a closed embedding 
\begin{equation}\label{closedembedding}
    \iota_{\mfw_J, \mfw} \colon Z_{\mfw_J} \rightarrow Z_{\mfw}
\end{equation} 
In particular, for $1 \leq m \leq n$ we can take $\mfw[m] := (s_{i_1}, \ldots, s_{i_m})$ which is $\mfw_J$ for $J = (i_1,\ldots,i_m) \subset (i_1,\ldots,i_n) = I$, and we denote the morphism given by projection to the first $m$ coordinates by
\begin{equation}\label{fibration}
    \psi_{\mfw, m} \colon Z_{\mfw} \rightarrow Z_{\mfw[m]}
\end{equation}
For $m = n-1$ we give it the special notation $\psi_{\mfw}$. Note then that
\[\psi_{\mfw, m} = \psi_{\mfw[m+1]}\circ\ldots \circ\psi_{\mfw[n-1]}\circ \psi_{\mfw}\]
since, for example $\psi_{\mfw[n-1]} \colon Z_{\mfw[n-1]} \to Z_{\mfw[n-2]}$.
The following proposition is given in \cite{Brion2004}.
\begin{prop}
    The projection $\psi_{\mfw}$ is a locally trivial $\bbp^1$-fibration and therefore $\psi_{\mfw,m}$ is smooth. We also have the following fiber diagram where $\mfv := \mfw[n-1]$ and $f_{i} := G/B \to G/P_{i}$ is the standard projection.
    \[\begin{tikzcd}
	{Z_{\mathfrak{w}}} & {G/B} \\
	{Z_{\mathfrak{v}}} & {G/P_{i_n}}
	\arrow["{\theta_{\mathfrak{w}}}", from=1-1, to=1-2]
	\arrow["{\psi_{\mathfrak{w}}}"', from=1-1, to=2-1]
	\arrow["f_{i_n}", from=1-2, to=2-2]
	\arrow["{f_{i_n}\circ \theta_{\mathfrak{v}}}"', from=2-1, to=2-2]
\end{tikzcd}\]
\end{prop}
We work out the diagram to familiarize the reader with the definitions. Clockwise, it reads $f_{i_n} \circ \theta_{\mfw}[p_1,\ldots,p_n] = f_{i_n}(p_1\cdots p_n B) = p_1\cdots p_{n-1} P_{i_n}$. Counterclockwise, we get $f_{i_n} \circ \theta_{\mfv} \circ \psi_{\mfw}[p_1,\ldots,p_n] = f_{i_n} \circ \theta_{\mfv}[p_1,\ldots,p_{n-1}] = f_{i_n}(p_1\cdots p_{n-1} B) = p_1\cdots p_{n-1} P_{i_n}$ which is equivalent.

The fibration also admits a section
\begin{equation}\label{section}
    \sigma_\mfw \colon Z_{\mfv} \to Z_\mfw, \quad [p_1,\ldots,p_{n-1}] \mapsto [p_1,\ldots,p_{n-1},1]
\end{equation}

Let $\mfw(j) := (s_{i_1}, \ldots, \hat{s}_{i_j}, \ldots, s_{i_n})$ for $j \in I$ and define the "boundary" $\del Z_{\mfw}$ of $Z_{\mfw}$ by
\[\del Z_{\mfw} := \bigcup_{j=1}^n Z_{\mfw(j)}\]
with the closed reduced subscheme structure, where $Z_{\mfw(j)}$ are seen to be divisors of $Z_{\mfw}$ via the closed embedding $\iota_{\mfw(j), \mfw}$ in (\ref{closedembedding}). Further they are the irreducible components of $\del Z_{\mfw}$ such that for any $J \subset I$, as schemes
\[Z_{\mfw_J} \simeq \bigcap_{j \not \in J} Z_{\mfw(j)}\]
In particular, $\interior Z_{\mfw} = Z_{\mfw} \setminus \del Z_{\mfw}$.

Recall we can construct a line bundle on $G/B$ as follows. We can identify the characters $\bbx^{\bullet}(B)$ on $B$ with $\weight$ on the torus $T$ by restriction, so given a character $\lambda \in \weight$ let $k_{\lambda}$ denote the corresponding one-dimensional representation of $B$. Then we have a $G$-equivariant line bundle
\[\fanl(\lambda) := G \times_B k_{-\lambda} \rightarrow G/B\]
associated to the principal $B$-bundle $\pi_B \colon G \to G/B$ via $k_{-\lambda}$. In fact all line bundles are of this form, and in particular 
\[\omega_{G/B} \simeq \fanl(-2\rho)\]
where $\rho := \chi_1 + \ldots + \chi_{\ell}$ is the sum of the fundamental weights which form a basis of $\weight$, and equivalently the half-sum of the positive roots. More generally if $V$ is a finite $B$-module we define $\fanl(V) := G \times_B V \to G/B$ as the associated vector bundle.

We define $\fanl_{\mfw}(\lambda)$ as the pullback of $\fanl(\lambda)$ on $G/B$ along $\theta_{\mfw}$. More generally for a finite $B$-module $V$ define $\fanl_{\mfw}(V)$ as the pullback of $\fanl(V)$ along $\theta_{\mfw}$.

\begin{prop}
    Let $\mfw = (s_{i_1}, \ldots, s_{i_n})$ be any sequence of simple reflections in $W$. Then the canonical bundle $\omega_{Z_{\mfw}}$ of $Z_{\mfw}$ is given by 
    \begin{equation}\label{eq1}
        \omega_{Z_{\mfw}} \simeq \calo_{Z_{\mfw}}(-\del Z_{\mfw}) \otimes \fanl_{\mfw}(-p)
    \end{equation}
    and as $B$-equivariant line bundles,
    \begin{equation}\label{eq2}
        \omega_{Z_{\mfw}} \simeq \calo_{Z_{\mfw}}(-\del Z_{\mfw}) \otimes \fanl_{\mfw}(-p) \otimes \mathbf{k}_{-\rho}
    \end{equation}
    where $\mathbf{k}_{-\rho}$ is the trivial line bundle on $Z_{\mfw}$ equipped with the $B$-equivariant line bundle structure coming from the representation $k_{-\rho}$ of $B$.
\end{prop}

The proof given in \cite{Brion2004} is enlightening so we include it here.
\begin{proof}
    We proceed by induction on $\ell(\mfw) = n$. For $n = 1$, $Z_{\mfw} = P_i/B \simeq \bbp^1$ and hence $\omega_{Z_{\mfw}} \simeq \calo_{\bbp^1}(-2x_0)$ for any $x_0 \in \bbp^1$. It's easy to see $\fanl_{\mfw}(-\rho) \simeq \calo_{\bbp^1}(-x_0)$. Then (\ref{eq1}) follows for $n = 1$.

    Recall the $\bbp^1$-fibration $\psi_{\mfw} \colon Z_{\mfw} \to Z_\mfv$ in (\ref{fibration}) and section $\sigma_\mfw$ in (\ref{section}). By induction, suppose the validity of (\ref{eq1}) for $Z_\mfv$. The line bundle $\fanl_\mfw(\rho)$ is of degree $1 = \langle \rho, \alpha^\vee_i \rangle$ along the fibers $P_i/B \cong \bbp^1$ of $\psi_\mfw$. Then 
    \[\sigma_\mfw^* \fanl_\mfw(\rho) = \fanl_\mfv(\rho)\]
hence
\begin{equation}\label{fanl}
\fanl_\mfw(\rho)
\;\simeq\;
\calo_{Z_{\mfw}}\bigl(\sigma_{\mfw}(Z_{\mfv})\bigr)
\;\otimes\;
\psi_{\mfw}^*\,\fanl_{\mfv}(\rho).
\end{equation}
Since 
\(\partial Z_{\mfw}
=\psi_{\mfw}^{-1}(\partial Z_{\mfv})\;\cup\;\sigma_{\mfw}(Z_{\mfv})\),
we also have
\begin{equation}\label{partial}
\calo_{Z_{\mfw}}\bigl(-\partial Z_{\mfw}\bigr)
\;\simeq\;
\calo_{Z_{\mfw}}\bigl(-\sigma_{\mfw}(Z_{\mfv})\bigr)
\;\otimes\;
\psi_{\mfw}^*\,\calo_{Z_{\mfv}}\bigl(-\partial Z_{\mfv}\bigr).
\end{equation}

\smallskip

\noindent
By the inductive hypothesis,
\[
\omega_{Z_{\mfv}}
\;\simeq\;
\calo_{Z_{\mfv}}\bigl(-\partial Z_{\mfv}\bigr)
\;\otimes\;
\fanl_{\mfv}(-\rho),
\]
and since for any \(\bbp^1\)–bundle \(\pi\colon X\to Y\) one has
\(\omega_{X}\simeq\omega_{X/Y}\otimes\pi^*\omega_Y\) with
\(\omega_{X/Y}\simeq\calo_X(-2\,\sigma(Y))\),
we deduce
\[
\begin{aligned}
\omega_{Z_{\mfw}}
&\;\simeq\;
\calo_{Z_{\mfw}}\bigl(-2\,\sigma_{\mfw}(Z_{\mfv})\bigr)
\;\otimes\;
\psi_{\mfw}^*
\Bigl(
\calo_{Z_{\mfv}}(-\partial Z_{\mfv})
\otimes
\fanl_{\mfv}(-\rho)
\Bigr)
\\
&\;\simeq\;
\Bigl[
\calo_{Z_{\mfw}}\bigl(-\sigma_{\mfw}(Z_{\mfv})\bigr)
\otimes
\psi_{\mfw}^*\calo_{Z_{\mfv}}(-\partial Z_{\mfv})
\Bigr]
\;\otimes\;
\Bigl[
\calo_{Z_{\mfw}}\bigl(-\sigma_{\mfw}(Z_{\mfv})\bigr)
\otimes
\psi_{\mfw}^*\fanl_{\mfv}(-\rho)
\Bigr]
\\
&\;\simeq\;
\underbrace{\calo_{Z_{\mfw}}(-\partial Z_{\mfw})}_{(\ref{partial})}
\;\otimes\;
\underbrace{\fanl_{\mfw}(-\rho)}_{(\ref{fanl})}.
\end{aligned}
\]
By induction, we have shown (\ref{eq1}) holds.

Since $Z_\mfw$ is projective, for any two $B$-equivariant bundles $\fanl_1, \fanl_2$ isomorphic as nonequivariant bundles, there exists a character $\lambda \in \weight^+$ such that $\fanl_1 \simeq \fanl_2 \otimes \bfk_{\lambda}$.
\end{proof}

As a corollary we get the following theorem.

\begin{thm}
    $Z_{\mfw}$ is split by $\sigma^{p-1}$ where $\sigma \in H^0(Z_{\mfw}, \omega_{Z_{\mfw}}^{-1})$ vanishes on all the divisors $Z_{\mfw(j)}$ for $1 \leq j \leq n$. Thus for any $J \subset I$, $Z_{\mfw_J}$ is compatibly split by $\sigma^{p-1}$ where $Z_{\mfw_J}$ is identified with its image under the closed embedding $\iota_{\mfw_J, \mfw}$ in (\ref{closedembedding}).
\end{thm}
\begin{proof}
    This is essentially a direct application of Proposition \ref{bkprop1.3.11}.
\end{proof}

\section{Frobenius Splitting of $\Bun_G$}

Recall our definition of the Ran space of a reduced connected curve $X$ over $k$. 
\[\Ran(X)(R) = \varinjlim\nolimits_{I} X^I(R)\]
It suffices to show that the Frobenius morphism $F \colon \Ran(X) \to \Ran(X)$ is split. In fact, we believe the stronger statement that $F_* \calo_{\Ran(X)} = \calo_{\Ran(X)}$. That is, for all $x\colon \spec R \to \Ran(X)$ we want to show that if the following is a fiber product diagram
\[\begin{tikzcd}
	Z & {\mathrm{Ran}(X)} \\
	{\mathrm{Spec}\, R} & {\mathrm{Ran}(X)}
	\arrow[from=1-1, to=1-2]
	\arrow[from=1-1, to=2-1]
	\arrow["\lrcorner"{anchor=center, pos=0.125}, draw=none, from=1-1, to=2-2]
	\arrow["F", from=1-2, to=2-2]
	\arrow["x"', from=2-1, to=2-2]
\end{tikzcd}\]
then $\Gamma(Z, \calo_Z) \simeq R$. Suppose first that $R = k$ and $x \colon \spec k \to \Ran(X)$ is given by $x \in X(k)$. The $A$-valued points $Z(A)$ are given by $\spec A \to Z$, hence by the universal property by maps $\spec A \to \spec k$ and $\spec A \to \Ran(X)$ that commute with $x,F$.
\[\begin{tikzcd}
	{\mathrm{Spec}\,A} \\
	& Z & {\mathrm{Ran}(X)} \\
	& {\mathrm{Spec}\, k} & {\mathrm{Ran}(X)}
	\arrow[from=1-1, to=2-2]
	\arrow["y", curve={height=-12pt}, from=1-1, to=2-3]
	\arrow["x_A", curve={height=12pt}, from=1-1, to=3-2, swap]
	\arrow[from=2-2, to=2-3]
	\arrow[from=2-2, to=3-2]
	\arrow["\lrcorner"{anchor=center, pos=0.125}, draw=none, from=2-2, to=3-3]
	\arrow["F", from=2-3, to=3-3]
	\arrow["x"', from=3-2, to=3-3]
\end{tikzcd}\]
In the above diagram, $y = \{y_i \in X(A) \mid i \in I\} \in X^I(A)$ for some finite nonempty $I$. By commutativity, 
\[x_A = Fy = \{F(y_i) \in X(A) \mid i \in I\} \in X(A)\]
Let $F^I \colon X^I \to X^I$ be the component-wise Frobenius, and define $Z^I(A) := (F^I)^{-1}(x_A)$ where we denote the constant point $x_A = \{x_i = x_A \in X(A) \mid i \in I\} \in X^I(A)$. Then it is obvious from the above description that $Z = \varinjlim_I Z^I$. 

Suppose $X$ is \textit{smooth} so that \'etale locally around $x$, we can pick a uniformizer $t$ such that $\hat{\calo}_{X,x}\simeq k[[t]]$ and $F^{\#}(t) = t^p$. Then for $I = \{1,\ldots,n\}$, the fiber has complete local ring 
\[\hat{\calo}_{Z^I} \simeq k[[t_1,\ldots,t_n]]/(t_1^p, \ldots,t_n^p) 
\simeq k[t_1,\ldots,t_n]/(t_1^p, \ldots, t_n^p)\]
over the unique closed point. Since $Z^I$ is zero dimensional this equals its ring of global sections.
\[\calo_{Z_I} \simeq k[t_1,\ldots,t_n]/(t_1^p, \ldots, t_n^p)\]
Given a surjection $\phi \colon J \twoheadrightarrow I$, the corresponding diagonal map $\Delta(\phi) \colon X^I \to X^J$ carries the fibers of $F^I$ to the fibers of $F^J$.
\[\Delta(\phi)(F^I)^{-1}(x_1,\ldots,x_n) = \Delta(\phi)(F^{-1}(x_1),\ldots,F^{-1}(x_n)) = (F^{-1}(x_{\phi(1)}), \ldots, F^{-1}(x_{\phi(m)}))\]
\[(F^J)^{-1}(x_{\phi(1)}, \ldots, x_{\phi(m)}) = (F^{-1}(x_{\phi(1)}), \ldots, F^{-1}(x_{\phi(m)}))\]
and therefore we have diagonal maps
\[\Delta(\phi) \colon Z^I \to Z^J\]
On the level of functions
\[\Delta(\phi)^{\#} \colon \hat{\calo}_{Z^J} \to \hat{\calo}_{Z^I}\]
\[ k[t_1,\ldots,t_m]/(t_1^p, \ldots,t_m^p) \to  k[t_1,\ldots,t_n]/(t_1^p, \ldots,t_n^p)\]
sending $t_j \mapsto t_{\phi(j)}$. Then
\[\varprojlim\nolimits_I \Gamma(Z^I, \calo_{Z^I}) = \{(f_I) \in \prod_{I} k[t_1,\ldots,t_n]/(t_1^p, \ldots, t_n^p) \mid \Delta(\phi)^{\#}(f_J) = f_I\}\]
Suppose $f_1(t_1)$. Then $\phi \colon \{1,2\} \to \{1\}$ implies $f_2(t_1,t_1) = f_1(t_1)$.
